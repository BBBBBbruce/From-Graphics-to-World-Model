\chapter*{How to Read This Book}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{How to Read This Book}\markboth{How to Read This Book}{}
A robot must recognize a cup, move it without knocking it over, and recover from a failed grasp. This book explains that problem and helps you choose a precise research question.

You should be comfortable with vectors, basic derivatives, and short Python programs. Chapter~\ref{ch:foundations} reviews the probability and learning concepts used later. No previous robotics or reinforcement-learning course is required. Advanced policy-gradient details are identified explicitly; the first pass can focus on the meaning of each quantity.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=4pt]
\item \textbf{From images to predictions.} Begin with rendering, then distinguish physical state, observation, action, and uncertainty.
\item \textbf{Policies and demonstrations.} Learn how a distribution generates continuous actions and how demonstration context changes behavior.
\item \textbf{World models and improvement.} Connect predicted experience to rewards, values, and policy updates. On a first pass, Sections~\ref{sec:policy-gradient}--\ref{sec:fpo} and Appendix~\ref{app:bounds} may be postponed.
\item \textbf{Controllable prediction.} Specify commands, align data, and preserve the information needed near contact.
\item \textbf{Evidence of improvement.} Separate prediction tests, decision tests, and closed-loop evaluation.
\item \textbf{A first research direction.} Run the small experiment, diagnose its failure, and choose a question that matches your interests and resources.
\end{enumerate}

A cup-moving task introduces the ideas. A connector-insertion task makes hidden contact and small geometric errors visible. These are teaching examples, not reports of experiments. Numbers described as illustrative are chosen for explanation. The laptop experiment is a separate executable toy system; its results do not establish robot performance.

Read each figure before its explanation. Try the exercises before consulting Appendix~\ref{app:solutions}. The symbol list provides one notation system; symbols are also defined locally. A hat marks a prediction or estimate, not a new measurement.

References use author--year citations and link to primary sources. For conference and journal papers, the year names the cited publication. For preprints, the recorded year and version identify the inspected edition. A later file revision is not a new conference publication. The text distinguishes published mechanisms, teaching constructions, and proposals. Research proposals require experiments before they can support performance claims.
