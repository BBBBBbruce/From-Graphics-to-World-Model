\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}\markboth{Introduction}{}
This book is for students and professionals with a background in computer graphics, physics, mathematics, or a related quantitative field who want to move into robotics, particularly world models for robot learning. It connects familiar ideas to current research and helps you choose what to study or investigate next.

Graphics gives us ways to represent scenes and form images. Physics describes how systems change under forces and constraints. Mathematics provides tools for reasoning about uncertainty, learning, and optimization. Robotics brings these ideas together around a decision: what should an agent do, given what it can observe and what it wants to achieve?

Consider a robot moving a cup onto a coaster. A good image of the cup does not tell us whether a grasp will hold. A plausible video of a successful lift does not tell us whether the proposed commands would produce it. To choose useful actions, the robot needs a model that responds to those actions and preserves the consequences that matter. To learn from that model, it also needs a way to recognize when its predictions are wrong.

We begin with scene representations and the distinction between observing a state and predicting a transition. We then study policies that generate movements, demonstrations that specify tasks, and rewards that guide learning. The later chapters examine controllable prediction, contact, and the experiments needed to judge whether a model improves decisions. The final chapter develops possible learning and research directions, from a small dynamics experiment to comparisons of published methods.

You should be comfortable with linear algebra, multivariable calculus, probability, basic machine learning, and Python at roughly third-year undergraduate level or above. Equivalent preparation through professional work or self-study is welcome. No previous robotics experience is required. If the mathematical refresher in Chapter~\ref{ch:foundations} is familiar, focus on how the prediction problem changes when actions enter the picture.

Follow the cup and connector examples through the chapters, work through the derivations, and use the exercises to test your understanding. For a hands-on starting point, Chapter~\ref{ch:directions} includes a small experiment that runs on a laptop. Its simple response law makes it possible to examine why a model chooses the wrong action before tackling the same question in a visual world model.
